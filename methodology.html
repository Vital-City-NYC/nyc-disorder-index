How the disorder index works

Every source, every grouping, every formula and every known limitation. Nothing here is a black box.

What this is

The disorder index tracks ten strands of everyday street disorder in New York City's 59 community districts, using the city's record of complaints to 311, the non-emergency service line. For each strand and each district it reports the direction of change (the past 12 months against the 12 before), the district's standing relative to the rest of the city (reports per 1,000 residents) and a monthly trend line back to January 2021.

It deliberately does not compute a single composite disorder score. The strands are measured with different reliability, reflect different behaviors and would require arbitrary weights to combine. A neighborhood that scored "62" would invite false precision; a neighborhood that is loud but clean would disappear into the average. We report the strands side by side and let the pattern speak.

The ten strands

Each strand is the sum of specific, exact 311 complaint types. A report counts toward a strand if and only if its complaint type appears below. The groupings are editorial choices; this table is the complete definition.

The strands are presented as equals even though their volumes differ enormously — over the past 12 months, noise generated about 46 times as many reports as graffiti (791,202 versus 17,212 citywide). That is deliberate: call volume reflects how reportable a problem is, not how much it matters.

The conditions layer: reports versus inspections

The single biggest limitation of a 311-based index is that it measures reporting, not conditions — a quiet neighborhood might have plenty of rats and few people who call. For two strands, the city runs an inspection program that lets us check the reports against what an inspector actually found. These appear on the relevant cards as a separate, blue-tinted panel, kept visually and methodologically apart from the 311 data.

StrandInspection sourceWhat we measure
Rats and rodentsDOHMH Rodent Inspection (initial inspections only)The share of initial inspections that found active rat signs (result "Failed for Rat Activity"). Because it is inspectors looking rather than residents calling, it is an independent read on where rats actually are. We report it per district with the same 12-months-vs-prior-12 trend and 1-of-59 ranking as the 311 strands. A district needs at least 30 inspections in each window to be given a trend.
Streets in disrepairDOT Street Pavement RatingsThe length-weighted share of a district's rated roadway in poor condition (a rating below 4 on DOT's 1–10 scale, where 10 is best), plus the average rating. Ratings are done on a rolling multi-year cycle, so this is a condition snapshot — we keep each segment's most recent rating from 2023 onward — not a 12-month trend. It is meant to be read against the report levels, not their direction.

The point of the panel is the comparison, not agreement. Where reports and inspections diverge sharply — as in Elmhurst and South Corona, which sits near the bottom of the city in 311 rat complaints but near the top in inspector-found rat activity — the card says so plainly, because the gap between what a neighborhood reports and what is actually there is itself one of the most useful things this tool can show.

Two honest caveats on this layer. Rodent inspections are geolocated by latitude and longitude and assigned to a community district by point-in-polygon; about 8 percent of inspections fall in parks, on the water's edge or at bad coordinates and are left out of district figures (the citywide rate includes every geocoded inspection and matches a direct query of the source exactly). And an "inspection" is not a neutral sample of the whole neighborhood — the Health Department inspects where it has reason to, often following complaints or known problem blocks — so the inspection rate reflects conditions among inspected properties, not a random audit of every building.

Resident voices

Alongside the data, a selected district's story carries short quotes from residents interviewed about quality of life in their neighborhood. They come from an interview program compiled by Vital City — not a public dataset. We received 211 quotes across 68 neighborhoods, interviewed between January and July 2026.

Two rules govern what appears. First, scope: this index tracks disorder, not crime, so we show only the 125 quotes that touch a strand tracked here — homelessness, rats, dirty streets, drugs and public drinking, public urination, noise or panhandling. The 86 quotes that were solely about crime or gun violence are set aside. Each shown quote is tagged, by keyword, with the strand or strands it mentions. Second, geography: each quote is matched to the resident's stated neighborhood and then to that neighborhood's primary community district. Some neighborhoods straddle two districts and are assigned to one, so treat the placement as approximate.

Quotes appear verbatim, newest first, with attribution exactly as the interview program provided it — a first name where one was given, otherwise age, race and gender. They are lived impressions, not measurements: a resident saying the rats are everywhere is evidence about perception, which can run ahead of or behind what 311 reports and city inspections show. That gap is the point. The voices sit beside the numbers so the two can be read against each other.

Geography

The unit is the community district, using the 311 record's community_board field, which the city assigns from the incident address. The 59 real community districts are included; records assigned to parks and other joint interest areas (such as Central Park) or marked "Unspecified" are excluded from district figures but included in citywide totals. In the analysis window, 2.6 percent of tracked reports could not be assigned to a district.

Population denominators come from the 2020 decennial census: total population of each census tract, summed into community district tabulation areas, City Planning's tract-built approximations of the community districts. The 59 districts account for 8,803,186 of the city's 8,804,190 residents; the remainder live in park tracts. Denominators are held fixed at 2020 throughout, so multi-year rate changes partly reflect population change. This matters most in fast-growing districts.

Calculations

Trend

For each district and strand: total reports in the 12 months July 2025 through June 2026, compared with July 2024 through June 2025. Percent change = (recent − prior) ÷ prior × 100. Comparing full 12-month windows removes seasonality — a January is always weighed against a January. A strand is labeled rising at +10 percent or more, falling at −10 percent or less and steady in between. Where the prior window had fewer than 30 reports, no trend is called: percentage swings on tiny bases are noise.

Standing

For each strand, districts are ranked by reports per 1,000 residents over the past 12 months and divided into thirds: the 20 districts with the most reports per resident, the middle 19 and the 20 with the fewest. We use thirds rather than precise ranks or scores in the display because the underlying data does not support finer distinctions — though the exact rank (1 to 59) is shown for orientation.

Concentration flag

A known artifact of 311 data is the prolific address: a single building or corner (or a single determined caller) that generates enough reports to move a whole district's trend. For each district and strand we compute reports per distinct incident address over the past 12 months. Where that ratio is at least three times the citywide ratio for the same strand and the district logged at least 100 reports, the card carries a flag: a small number of addresses accounts for an outsized share. The flag is a caution, not a correction — the reports are real, but the trend may not describe the whole neighborhood.

One technical note: for strands built from more than one complaint type, distinct-address counts are computed per complaint type and summed, so an address that generates two different complaint types is counted twice in the denominator. This makes the reports-per-address ratio an approximation rather than a true distinct-address count. Because the same method is applied to both a district and the citywide baseline it is compared against, the effect on the ratio is small; the four single-type strands (rats, illegal dumping, graffiti, panhandling) are exact. The flag is meant as a caution to investigate, not a precise statistic.

What we deliberately left out, and why

Candidate sourceWhy it is not here
Criminal summonses (New York Police Department)Summons counts track where officers are deployed and what they choose to enforce at least as much as where disorder occurs. A neighborhood can look worse simply because it got more enforcement attention. We may add enforcement data later as a clearly separated layer, never blended into the strands.
911 calls for serviceA promising future addition — calling 911 is costlier for the caller than 311, so it may track genuine problems more tightly. But the public dataset is organized by police precinct, which does not align with community districts, and mixing geographies would quietly corrupt comparisons. Held for a future version.
Subway fare evasionMeasured through enforcement and inspection, with the same deployment bias as summonses, and not published at neighborhood level.
Storefront vacanciesThe public registry data lags badly — the most recent reliable figures are years old — and this tool is built to be refreshed monthly.
Mayor's Management Report cleanliness scorecardsPublished on a long lag, with neighborhood-to-neighborhood variation too small to rank meaningfully (most districts score above 90 percent acceptably clean).
Sanitation "Scorecard" street-cleanliness ratingsThis would be the ideal inspection cross-check for the dirty-streets strand — inspectors rating whether streets are acceptably clean. But the Open Data feed (rqhp-hivt) has not been updated since October 2023 and appears discontinued, so there is no current observational measure of street cleanliness to add.
Syringes collected in parks; restaurant rodent violationsConsidered as further inspection cross-checks (for public drug use and for rats). Syringe collection is confined to parks and depends on where outreach programs operate, so it is not a fair district-to-district measure; restaurant rodent violations are indoor and commercial, and the direct rodent inspections already used are a cleaner signal. Both are held.

Limitations, plainly

This measures reporting, not prevalence. Every number is a count of complaints, which requires a resident who noticed, cared and knew how to call. Neighborhoods differ systematically in all three. Comparisons between districts are therefore softer than comparisons of the same district over time, which is why the trend gets top billing.

Reporting propensity can itself trend. A 311 outreach campaign, a new app feature or a neighborhood group's push can raise reports with no change in conditions. The concentration flag catches the single-address version of this; it cannot catch a diffuse shift in habits.

Complaint categories change. Agencies occasionally rename or split 311 complaint types. The window starts in January 2021 partly to stay within a stable stretch of the current taxonomy. If a category changes, this page's definitions will be updated and noted.

Population denominators are fixed at April 2020. See geography, above.

June 2026 is the freshest month shown and was pulled two days after it ended. 311 records are created at intake, so late-arriving records are rare but not impossible.

Reproducibility

The pipeline is a set of short Python scripts in the project repository: scripts/build_populations.py (census tract populations into district denominators), scripts/build_data.py (311 aggregates into data/disorder_data.json, the file the page reads), and scripts/build_conditions.py (rodent-inspection and pavement data into data/conditions_data.json, the conditions layer). A fourth script, scripts/verify.py, independently re-queries NYC Open Data with different query shapes and diffs every published number against the source. All use only documented public endpoints, run in a couple of minutes and print citywide totals for cross-checking against the published page. Re-running them each month refreshes the entire product.

Questions, corrections or a strand we should add? This is a first measure — the best available from administrative data, not the last word.
