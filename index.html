The disorder index

Ten everyday signals of street disorder — noise, rats, trash, graffiti and more — tracked neighborhood by neighborhood from what New Yorkers report.

Loading data…

Crime statistics get headlines, but much of what shapes how a block feels day to day never becomes a crime statistic: the party that goes until 4 a.m., the rat in the tree pit, the mattress dumped on the corner, the encampment by the underpass. This page tracks ten of those signals — we call them strands of disorder — using the city's own record of what residents report to 311, the city's non-emergency complaint line.

There is no single disorder score here, on purpose. A neighborhood can be quiet but dirty, or loud but spotless, and one number would bury that. Instead, for each strand you get three plain facts: which direction it's heading (the past 12 months against the 12 before), how the neighborhood compares with the rest of the city (adjusted for population) and the trend over five and a half years. The fine print on every card says exactly which complaint types feed it.

One caveat above all: this is a record of reporting, not a census of disorder. Neighborhoods differ in how readily people call 311, and a jump can mean more problems or more willingness to report them. Where a handful of addresses drives a number, we flag it. The methodology spells out every choice and every limitation.

How we did this

All figures come from 311 Service Requests from 2010 to Present (NYC Open Data), covering January 2021 through June 2026 and accessed July 2, 2026. Population adjustments use the 2020 decennial census, summed from census tracts to community districts. Trends compare full 12-month windows, which removes seasonality. We deliberately excluded several tempting sources — criminal summonses, subway fare evasion, storefront vacancies, the Mayor's Management Report — and the full methodology explains why, along with every complaint-type grouping, every formula and every known limitation.
